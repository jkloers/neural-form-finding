\abstract{Kirigami sheets deploy from a flat, cut state into three-dimensional or
expanded planar configurations, which makes the cut pattern a design variable for
shape. Solving the inverse problem --- finding the pattern whose deployed
configuration approximates a prescribed target --- requires differentiating a
target-matching objective through the mechanics of deployment, and the mechanics
are dominated by the slender ligaments that join the panels. These ligaments
yield, buckle out of plane, and eventually tear, so the ideal-hinge and
linear-spring idealisations commonly used in kirigami form finding are not
predictive at the deployment amplitudes of interest. We present an end-to-end
differentiable pipeline in which (i) the flat cut pattern is parameterised so that
non-self-intersection holds by construction, removing the need for a geometric
validity solver; (ii) deployment is a potential-energy minimisation over
rigid-panel degrees of freedom, differentiated through the implicit function
theorem rather than through the solver iterates; and (iii) each ligament is
represented by a learned condensed stored-energy function of its three
rigid-body-invariant deformation measures and its own geometry, trained on
three-dimensional elasto-plastic finite-element simulations of the ligament
neighbourhood. Because plastic softening can render the equilibrium Hessian
indefinite and annihilate the design gradient, the adjoint solve is
Tikhonov-regularised. We further report the calibration of the whole chain to a
physical $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ polyethylene terephthalate sheet, from tensile coupons
to single-ligament experiments, and show that the out-of-plane buckling amplitude
of a real ligament is predicted without material tuning.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Cutting a flat sheet along a prescribed pattern turns it into a mechanism. The
resulting kirigami tessellation is an assembly of nearly rigid panels connected by
the uncut strips of material left between the cuts, and it deploys --- expanding
in plane, or buckling out of it --- along a small number of kinematic modes fixed
by the cut topology. The cut pattern is therefore a design variable for shape:
choosing where to cut chooses what the sheet becomes when pulled. This makes
kirigami attractive wherever a flat, cheaply manufacturable stock must become a
prescribed curved or expanded configuration.
% CITE: kirigami metamaterials reviews; buckling-induced kirigami
% (Rafsanjani & Bertoldi, PRL 2017, arXiv:1702.06470); auxetic cut sheets.

The forward problem is well posed and largely understood: given a pattern, the
rigid-panel kinematics determine the family of admissible deployed
configurations. The inverse problem is not. One asks for the pattern whose
deployed configuration best approximates a prescribed target, and the map from
cuts to deployed shape passes through the mechanics of the ligaments; it is not
available in closed form. Gradient-based inverse design consequently requires
differentiating the target-matching objective through an equilibrium problem.

Two idealisations are usually made at this point, and both fail in the regime
where kirigami is actually useful. The first is to treat each ligament as an ideal
revolute joint, or as a linear torsional spring. Real ligaments are slender
elastic--plastic strips: they yield, they buckle out of plane --- which relieves
the fold moment by an order of magnitude and is the mechanism that makes large
deployment possible at all --- and they eventually tear. A constitutive
description that ignores these effects mispredicts both the deployed shape and the
onset of failure, and it cannot answer the question that governs the design, which
is how far the sheet may be deployed before the ligaments fail. The second
idealisation is to enforce the geometric admissibility of the flat pattern
(non-self-intersection, closure) as a constraint solved after the fact, which
inserts a second implicit solve into the gradient path.

This work removes both. We parameterise the flat closed sheet so that a valid,
non-self-intersecting pattern is produced for every admissible parameter value,
and we replace the ligament spring by a learned condensed stored-energy function
trained on three-dimensional elasto-plastic simulations of the ligament
neighbourhood. Deployment is then a potential-energy minimisation over the
rigid-panel degrees of freedom, and the design gradient is obtained from the
implicit function theorem applied to that minimisation. The whole chain --- cut
parameters, flat geometry, deployed equilibrium, target mismatch --- is a single
differentiable program.

The modelling hypotheses are stated and justified where they are used, and are
collected here for reference. \textbf{(H1)} The panels are rigid: all deformation
localises in the ligaments. \textbf{(H2)} The panels remain coplanar throughout
deployment, out-of-plane deformation being confined to the ligaments; the
condition under which this holds is derived in Section~\ref{sec:buckling-locality}
and is satisfied by the specimens tested here. \textbf{(H3)} The stored energy of
the assembly is a sum of independent ligament contributions. \textbf{(H4)} Each
ligament is memoryless: its energy depends on the current configuration relative
to the flat reference, not on the deformation path. \textbf{(H5)} Deployment is
quasi-static.

Section~\ref{sec:methods} develops the model: the tessellation and its design
parameters, the rigid-panel kinematics, the ligament deformation measures, the
energy functional and the equilibrium problem, the differentiable pipeline built
on them, the learned ligament energy, the finite-element oracle that trains it,
and the calibration of the chain to a physical sheet.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the resulting designs and their computational
cost. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} analyses when out-of-plane buckling localises
in the ligaments rather than tilting the panels, which is the condition (H2) rests
on. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The kirigami tessellation}

We model a planar kirigami metamaterial as an assembly of $n$ rigid polygonal
panels cut from a flat sheet and connected to one another, at their corners, by
slender elastic \emph{ligaments}. Throughout, \emph{hinge} denotes the joint
between two panels and \emph{ligament} the strip of uncut material that realises
it. Deformation is assumed to localise entirely in the ligaments and the panels
themselves are treated as rigid (H1). This reduction is appropriate in the regime
where the panels are much stiffer than the thin connecting ligaments, and it
collapses a high-dimensional continuum problem onto at most $3n$ degrees of
freedom. A tessellation is called \emph{deployable} if its rigid-panel kinematics
admit exactly one internal degree of freedom.

\paragraph{Topology.}
The connectivity is fixed by the cut pattern and does not change during
deformation. A cut pattern is the arrangement of slits that decomposes a
continuous sheet into rigid panels joined by intact ligaments, thereby fixing both
the connectivity and the flat reference geometry of the kirigami. We generate it
from the closed-state rigid-deployable planar quadrilateral kirigami (RDPQK)
construction of Dang \emph{et al.}, laid out on an $M \times N$ grid of unit
cells. % CITE: Dang et al. 2021, RDPQK closed-state construction.
From this pattern we derive, once and for all, the purely combinatorial data of
the assembly: the set of panels $\{P_\alpha\}_{\alpha=1}^{n}$, the corner (node)
incidences of each panel, and the set of hinges $\mathcal{H}$, where each hinge
$h=(i,k)\in\mathcal{H}$ identifies the pair of panel corners that an uncut strip
of material connects.

\paragraph{Geometry and design parameters.}
Each rigid panel $P_\alpha$ is described in a centroidal representation by its
centroid $\bm{c}_\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^2$ together with a set of
centroid-to-corner vectors $\{\bm{d}_{\alpha j}\}_j$; the corner positions in the
reference (flat, undeployed) configuration are
$\bm{x}_{\alpha j}^{0} = \bm{c}_\alpha + \bm{d}_{\alpha j}$. This reference state
is produced from the trainable design parameters
\begin{equation}
  \bm{\theta} = \bigl(\, \bm{z},\ \bm{\ell},\ \bm{w}_{\mathrm{lig}}\,\bigr),
  \qquad
  \bm{r} = \sigmoid(\bm{z}) \in (0,1)^{\lvert\mathrm{cuts}\rvert},
  \label{eq:design-params}
\end{equation}
where the per-cut logits $\bm{z}$ map through the logistic function to
\emph{aspect ratios} $\bm{r}$, each $r$ fixing the relative placement of a cut's
two endpoints; the ordered logits $\bm{\ell}$ map through a cumulative softmax to
the tangential positions $\bm{\tau}$ of the boundary cuts (\emph{boundary
sliders}); and $\bm{w}_{\mathrm{lig}}$ collects the per-hinge ligament widths.
Given the induced $(\bm{r},\bm{\tau})$, the interior cut vertices are obtained as
the solution of a sparse linear collinearity system
\begin{equation}
  \mathsf{A}(\bm{r})\,\bm{v} = \bm{b}(\bm{\tau}),
  \label{eq:collinearity}
\end{equation}
which places every interior vertex on the line through its neighbours with the
prescribed aspect ratio, while boundary vertices are pinned to the slider
positions. The map $\bm{\theta} \mapsto \{\bm{c}_\alpha, \bm{d}_{\alpha j}\}$ is
smooth, and the resulting flat tessellation is guaranteed to be
non-self-intersecting: the cut aspect ratios yield parallelogram voids, and the
boundary sliders are kept strictly ordered by the cumulative-softmax
parameterisation, so that the outline stays convex. The hypotheses of Tutte's
embedding theorem are then satisfied for every admissible $\bm{\theta}$.
% CITE: Tutte, How to draw a graph, 1963.
Validity of the flat state is therefore \emph{built in}, not enforced a
posteriori, and no geometric projection appears in the gradient path.

Alongside the shape parameters, each hinge $h$ carries three geometric attributes
that are constants of the deployment and enter the mechanics as descriptors of the
ligament: its rest orientation, summarised by the cut angle $\beta_h$ (a function
of the neighbouring aspect ratios $\bm{r}$); its ligament width
$w_{\mathrm{lig},h}$, which sets the local compliance; and the cut-tip fillet
ratio $\rho_{f}$, defined so that the fillet radius is $\rho_f\,\wlig$. The first
two are functions of $\bm{\theta}$ and are differentiable; the fillet ratio is
fixed by the cutting process and is not optimised. None of the three evolves
during deployment.

\subsection{Kinematics}

Because the panels are rigid, the configuration of the whole structure is
specified by three degrees of freedom per panel,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{q}_\alpha = (\bm{u}_\alpha,\ \phi_\alpha) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{S}^1,
  \qquad
  \bm{q} = (\bm{q}_1,\dots,\bm{q}_{n}) \in \mathbb{R}^{3n},
\end{equation}
a rigid-body translation $\bm{u}_\alpha$ and a rotation $\phi_\alpha$ of panel
$P_\alpha$ about its centroid. The displacement of corner $j$ of panel $\alpha$
follows exactly from the rigid-body map,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\bm{x}_{\alpha j}(\bm{q}_\alpha)
    = \bm{u}_\alpha + \bigl(R(\phi_\alpha) - I\bigr)\,\bm{d}_{\alpha j},
\end{equation}
where $R(\phi)$ is the planar rotation matrix. The rotation is kept fully
nonlinear --- no small-angle linearisation of the panel motion --- so that the
model remains valid through the large panel rotations that characterise
deployment.

\subsection{Hinge deformation measures}

The mechanical state of a hinge is captured by the relative motion of the two
panels it connects, expressed in a frame that is invariant to rigid-body motion of
the assembly. For hinge $h=(i,k)$, joining panels $i$ and $k$ with reference bond
vector $\bm{e}_h^{0} = \bm{x}_k^{0} - \bm{x}_i^{0}$ and unit bond direction
$\hat{\bm{e}}_h = \bm{e}_h^{0}/\lVert\bm{e}_h^{0}\rVert$, we remove the mean
rotation of the two adjacent panels --- a corotational, total-Lagrangian frame ---
and read off three scalar deformation measures,
\begin{equation}
  \epsh_h(\bm{q}) = \bigl(a_h,\ s_h,\ \vartheta_h\bigr),
  \label{eq:hinge-strain}
\end{equation}
namely an axial (opening) measure $a_h$ along $\hat{\bm{e}}_h$, a shear measure
$s_h$ transverse to it, and the relative rotation $\vartheta_h = \phi_k - \phi_i$.
By construction $\epsh_h$ vanishes under any rigid-body motion of the structure,
including arbitrarily large global rotations, and it depends only on the
\emph{current} configuration relative to the reference geometry: the ligament is
memoryless (H4). The triple $(a,s,\vartheta)$ is the minimal rigid-body-invariant
coordinate of the relative panel motion and is the argument of every constitutive
statement below.

\subsection{Energy functional}

We equip each hinge with a stored-energy function $W$ of its own deformation
measures and geometric descriptors,
\begin{equation}
  W_h = W\!\bigl(\epsh_h;\ \bm{g}_h\bigr),
  \qquad
  \bm{g}_h = \bigl(w_{\mathrm{lig},h},\ \beta_h,\ \rho_f\bigr),
  \label{eq:hinge-energy}
\end{equation}
and take the total elastic energy of the assembly to be the sum of independent
hinge contributions (H3),
\begin{equation}
  U(\bm{q};\,\bm{\theta}) = \sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}
    W\!\bigl(\epsh_h(\bm{q});\ \bm{g}_h\bigr).
  \label{eq:total-energy}
\end{equation}
We deliberately leave the constitutive form of $W$ abstract. Any objective,
frame-invariant function of $\epsh=(a,s,\vartheta)$, parameterised by the hinge
descriptors $\bm{g}_h$, is admissible, and different choices --- from a simple
quadratic spring to the data-driven surrogate of
Section~\ref{sec:surrogate} --- can be substituted without altering the
surrounding kinematics or topology. The only requirements are that $W$ be
non-negative, minimised at the undeformed state, and twice differentiable in both
its kinematic arguments and its geometric descriptors, the second derivative being
required for the tangent stiffness of the equilibrium solve.

\subsection{Equilibrium}

Deployment and loading are imposed through boundary conditions on the panel
degrees of freedom: a subset of panel degrees of freedom is prescribed (Dirichlet,
the clamped panels) and generalised forces $\bm{f}$ are applied to others
(Neumann). The mechanical state at load factor $\lambda\in[0,1]$ is the minimiser
of the total potential energy over the free degrees of freedom,
\begin{equation}
  \bm{q}^\star(\lambda;\,\bm{\theta})
    = \arg\min_{\bm{q}\,\in\,\mathcal{C}}
      \ \Pi(\bm{q};\,\bm{\theta},\lambda),
  \qquad
  \Pi = U(\bm{q};\,\bm{\theta}) - \lambda\,\bm{f}^\top \bm{q},
  \label{eq:equilibrium}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the affine space of configurations satisfying the
prescribed degrees of freedom. The load is applied incrementally in $\lambda$,
each increment warm-started from the previous equilibrium, so that the model
traces a quasi-static deformation path (H5) from the flat closed sheet to its
deployed configuration. Everything downstream of \eqref{eq:equilibrium} --- in
particular the equilibrium map $\bm{\theta} \mapsto \bm{q}^\star$ --- is
differentiable with respect to the design parameters $\bm{\theta}$, which is what
makes the object amenable to inverse design.

\subsection{Differentiable physics pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The pipeline is summarised in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}. The trainable parameters
are those of \eqref{eq:design-params},
$\bm{\theta}=(\bm{z},\bm{\ell},\bm{w}_{\mathrm{lig}})$. A differentiable forward
map --- the flat RDPQK closed-sheet builder --- assembles $\bm{r}$ and the
boundary sliders into the sparse collinearity system \eqref{eq:collinearity},
whose solution gives the flat panel vertices; non-self-intersection holds by
construction. Deployment is the static solve \eqref{eq:equilibrium}, with the
condensed hinge energy $W$ given by the learned surrogate of
Section~\ref{sec:surrogate}: its automatic-differentiation gradient
$\nabla_{\epsh} W=(F_a,F_s,M_\vartheta)$ supplies the internal generalised forces
that the solver balances, and $\nabla^2_{\epsh} W$ the tangent stiffness.

The minimisation is performed with a limited-memory BFGS solver under incremental
load stepping in $\lambda$, the load loop being unrolled as a scan primitive so
that the whole deployment remains a single compiled program. Gradients propagate
through the argument-of-the-minimum by the implicit function theorem rather than
through the solver iterates,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \bm{q}^\star}{\partial \bm{\theta}}
    = -\,H^{-1}\,\frac{\partial^2 \Pi}{\partial \bm{q}\,\partial \bm{\theta}},
  \qquad
  H = \nabla^2_{\bm{q}}\,\Pi ,
  \label{eq:ift}
\end{equation}
exposed as a custom vector--Jacobian product, so that the memory cost is
independent of the iteration count. Plastic softening in the surrogate can render
$H$ indefinite, in which case \eqref{eq:ift} does not return a descent direction
and the design gradient effectively vanishes; the backward solve is therefore
Tikhonov-regularised, $H\leftarrow H+\mu I$ with $\mu>0$, which restores a descent
direction at the cost of a controlled bias in the gradient. Finally the end-to-end
objective --- a Chamfer distance between the deployed panel centroids
$\{\bm{c}_\alpha(\bm{q}^\star)\}$ and the target point set $\mathcal{T}$, plus a
penalty on the predicted damage margin --- is minimised with Adam through a single
value-and-gradient evaluation, so that gradients flow
$\mathcal{L}\to\bm{q}^\star\to\Pi\to\bm{\theta}$ back to the cut aspect ratios,
boundary sliders, and ligament widths jointly. The implementation is written in
JAX and executed in double precision.

    \caption{The differentiable inverse-design pipeline. The design parameters
    $\bm{\theta}=(\bm{z},\bm{\ell},\bm{w}_{\mathrm{lig}})$ generate a flat,
    non-self-intersecting cut pattern; the pattern is deployed by minimising the
    total potential energy \eqref{eq:equilibrium} over the rigid-panel degrees of

    \eqref{eq:surrogate}; the deployed centroids are compared to the target, and

\subsection{Condensed hinge-energy surrogate}
\label{sec:surrogate}

Each ligament is replaced by a single learned stored-energy function
$W(\epsh;\bm{g})$, in $\mathrm{N\,mm}$. It condenses a full three-dimensional
elasto-plastic simulation into a scalar that the deployment solver can minimise,
and whose gradient is the internal generalised force the solver balances. Its
kinematic argument is the rigid-body-invariant triple $\epsh=(a,s,\vartheta)$ of
\eqref{eq:hinge-strain}. The design fixes the per-hinge descriptors
$\bm{g}=(\wlig,\beta,\rho_f)$ of \eqref{eq:hinge-energy}, with both $\beta$ and
the bond direction $\hat{\bm{e}}_h$ deterministic functions of the void aspect
ratios $\bm{r}$ and the boundary sliders. The geometry $\bm{g}(\bm{\theta})$ is
differentiable and is threaded through the solver as an \emph{explicit} argument,
so that the shape objective is minimised through the physics, including the effect
of the design on $\beta(\bm{\theta})$.

Let $\bm{\eta}:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}^m$ be a multilayer perceptron with
$\tanh$ activations acting on the standardised features
$\bm{\xi}(\epsh,\bm{g})=[\,a,\ s,\ \vartheta,\ \log\wlig,\ \beta,\ \rho_f\,]$.
The surrogate is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\,W(\epsh;\bm{g})
    = W_{s}\,\bigl\|\,\bm{\eta}\bigl(\bm{\xi}(\epsh,\bm{g})\bigr)
      -\bm{\eta}\bigl(\bm{\xi}(\bm{0},\bm{g})\bigr)\,\bigr\|_2^2\,},
  \label{eq:surrogate}
\end{equation}
with $W_s>0$ a learned energy scale. Two hidden layers of $64$ units are used
throughout. The squared form guarantees, by construction and without penalty
terms, that $W\geq 0$, that $W=0$ at the undeformed state --- hence that
rigid-body motions are free --- and that the tangent stiffness required by
\eqref{eq:ift} is smooth. The internal generalised force is the gradient
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{\epsh}W=(F_a,\,F_s,\,M_\vartheta),
\end{equation}
work-conjugate to $(a,s,\vartheta)$. A separate head predicts a continuous ductile
damage margin $D(\epsh;\bm{g})=\mathrm{softplus}(\cdot)\ge 0$, with $D\ge1$
denoting fracture; keeping it distinct from $W$ prevents the hard failure boundary
from distorting the smooth energy landscape.

The scale $W_{s}$ and the maps $\bm{\eta}$ and $D$ are fit to the finite-element
data set of Section~\ref{sec:rve} by a variance-normalised Sobolev loss,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}
  =\gamma\,\frac{\lVert W-W^\ast\rVert^2}{\sigma_W^2}
  +(1-\gamma)\,\frac{\lVert\nabla_{\epsh}W-\bm{F}^\ast\rVert^2}{\sigma_F^2}
  +w_D\,\lVert D-D^\ast\rVert^2,
  \label{eq:sobolev}
\end{equation}
in which starred quantities denote finite-element targets and $\sigma_W,\sigma_F$
their empirical standard deviations, so that the energy landscape and the
equilibrium-setting forces are matched on a common scale. The weight
$\gamma=0.65$ gives priority to the energy. The data are split by hinge geometry
rather than at random, so that the reported error measures extrapolation to unseen
ligaments.

\subsubsection{Finite-element model of the ligament}
\label{sec:rve}

The training data are generated by a three-dimensional finite-element model of a
representative volume element built around a single cut and truncated at a window
radius $r_{\mathrm{win}}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:hinge-layout}), solved with CalculiX. The
domain is discretised with quadratic 15-node pentahedral elements (C3D15) and the
analysis is geometrically nonlinear, so that yielding, hardening, and the
out-of-plane buckling of the ligament are all captured. Two material models are
used. The reference model is S235 structural steel, described by a finite-strain
$J_2$ (von~Mises) elastoplastic law with isotropic hardening
($E=210\,\mathrm{GPa}$, $\sigma_y\approx235\,\mathrm{MPa}$, tangent modulus
$E_t\approx2.1\,\mathrm{GPa}$). The second is the polyethylene terephthalate law
calibrated in Section~\ref{sec:calibration} and reported in
Table~\ref{tab:pet-params}, used for every comparison with experiment.

The representative volume element is loaded in a corotational frame by the three
relative panel-to-panel deformation measures $\epsh=(a,s,\vartheta)$, imposed as
kinematic boundary conditions on the two bounding panel faces, the far field being
truncated at $r_{\mathrm{win}}$ as discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. For each geometry $\bm{g}$ and each load state, the
simulation returns the condensed stored energy
\begin{equation}
  W(\epsh;\bm{g})=\int_\Omega \psi\,\mathrm{d}V ,
\end{equation}
where $\psi$ is the strain-energy density, together with its work-conjugate
generalised forces $\nabla_{\epsh}W=(F_a,F_s,M_\vartheta)$, the peak von~Mises
stress, the maximum out-of-plane displacement $u_z$, and the 99th percentile of
the equivalent plastic strain, $\varepsilon_p^{p99}$. Failure is measured by the
ductile damage ratio
\begin{equation}
  D=\frac{\varepsilon_p^{p99}}{\varepsilon_f(T)},
  \qquad
  \varepsilon_f(T)=\varepsilon_{f0}\exp\!\bigl[-k\,(T-\tfrac13)\bigr],
  \label{eq:damage}
\end{equation}
with $T=\sigma_m/\sigma_{\mathrm{eq}}$ the stress triaxiality and the locus
normalised so that $\varepsilon_f=\varepsilon_{f0}$ in uniaxial tension;
$D\geq1$ marks fracture. % CITE: Johnson-Cook / Rice-Tracey ductile failure locus.

\subsection{Calibration of the surrogate to a physical material}
\label{sec:calibration}

The surrogate of \eqref{eq:surrogate} is only as physical as the constitutive law
handed to the representative volume element that generates its training data. To
build and cut real specimens we therefore retarget the oracle from the reference
S235 steel to the sheet actually used in the experiments --- a $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$
biaxially oriented polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film --- through the
calibration chain
\begin{equation}
  \text{coupon}\ \longrightarrow\
  \text{bulk law}\ \longrightarrow\
  \text{RVE}\ \longrightarrow\
  W(\epsh;\bm{g})\ \longrightarrow\
  \text{deployment},
  \label{eq:calibration-chain}
\end{equation}
in which each arrow is identified or verified separately. The guiding principle is
a strict separation of \emph{material} from \emph{structure}: the bulk law is
identified from plain tensile coupons alone, and the ligament experiments are then
used to \emph{validate} the resulting predictions rather than to fit them. The
only genuinely structural quantity left free is the geometric imperfection
amplitude that seeds the out-of-plane buckling, which is not a material property
and cannot be measured on a coupon. This keeps the surrogate a prediction of the
ligament response rather than a curve fit to it.

\paragraph{Material identification.}
The sheet was identified from bulk indicators alone: a measured density
$\rho\simeq1390\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, which excludes PETG, polycarbonate and
poly(methyl methacrylate); a broad-band iridescence indicative of molecular
orientation; a $180^\circ$ fold without rupture; and a burn test in which the
specimen melted, dripped and drew into long filaments without sustained burning
--- the signature of a thermoplastic polyester, which also excludes poly(vinyl
chloride). Under load the film cold-draws through a propagating neck and becomes
birefringent, confirming strain-induced orientation.

\paragraph{Bulk law from tensile coupons.}
Ten rectangular strips following the ASTM~D882 geometry, six cut along the machine
direction and four across it, were pulled on a $5\,\mathrm{kN}$ single-column
frame at $1\,\mathrm{mm\,min^{-1}}$ with tape-tabbed flat grips; nine drew stably
and one failed prematurely at an edge flaw and was discarded. The yield stress is
direction-independent within scatter ($48.4\,\mathrm{MPa}$ machine direction
against $49.5\,\mathrm{MPa}$ cross direction, pooled
$48.8\pm1.6\,\mathrm{MPa}$), so the film is treated as in-plane isotropic and
described by a two-constant elastic law rather than by the nine-constant
orthotropic alternative.

Because the neck localises the deformation, the engineering plateau is not the
material law. The post-yield point is instead recovered from the measured natural
draw ratio $\Lambda=A_0/A_{\mathrm{drawn}}$, the ratio of the undeformed to the
drawn cross-section, through
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\mathrm{true}} = \Lambda\,\sigma_{\mathrm{plateau}},
  \qquad
  \varepsilon_{\mathrm{true}} = \ln\Lambda,
  \qquad
  \varepsilon_p = \ln\Lambda - \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{true}}}{E},
  \label{eq:draw-anchor}
\end{equation}
the last expression removing the elastic part to give the plastic strain that the
finite-element hardening table takes as input. With $\Lambda=3.28$ and an
engineering plateau of $33\,\mathrm{MPa}$, this places a large-strain anchor at
$109\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and $\varepsilon_p=1.15$. The neck halves the section at
constant volume, which confirms plastic incompressibility and hence the
admissibility of a $J_2$ flow rule. The resulting two-point monotonic hardening
table (Table~\ref{tab:pet-params}) replaces the bilinear steel law by a curve
anchored in the same large-strain regime the ligament actually experiences. No
softening branch is retained: a ligament loaded in bending forms a crease rather
than a propagating neck, so the relevant plastic response is monotonic.

\paragraph{What the coupons cannot deliver.}
Two entries of Table~\ref{tab:pet-params} are deliberately not taken from the
crosshead data. First, in the absence of an extensometer the crosshead
displacement contains the machine and grip compliance, which inflates the strain
and depresses the apparent modulus to $\approx1.2\,\mathrm{GPa}$, below the entire
physical range for PET; a literature value of $3.0\,\mathrm{GPa}$ is used instead.
This is inconsequential for the quantities the surrogate is calibrated against:
repeating a ligament opening simulation at $E=1.2$ and $3.0\,\mathrm{GPa}$ changes
the peak force by $2\%$ while the elastic stiffness scales with $E$, which
confirms that the peak is set by the yield stress, the hardening and the fracture
strain, and that adopting the compliance-corrupted modulus would merely transfer
machine compliance into the material law. Second, the specimens were stopped on
the draw plateau rather than broken, so $\varepsilon_{f0}$ is reported as a lower
bound; it is to be refined from the ligament tests, where fracture is the quantity
of interest, and the triaxiality exponent $k$ of \eqref{eq:damage} is likewise
carried as an assumed value pending the mixed-mode sweep described below. The
ductile-damage form \eqref{eq:damage} is itself supported by the conjugate
stress-whitening bands observed at $\pm45^\circ$ to the load axis: shear-driven
yielding on the planes of maximum shear rather than $90^\circ$ crazing, which
supports the von~Mises framework and indicates weak pressure sensitivity. Whitening
onset also provides a visible, photographable proxy for $D$, and thereby an
experimental anchor for the level of damage that constitutes functional failure.

\paragraph{Sweeping the deformation space with a uniaxial machine.}
A uniaxial testing frame cannot directly impose the three measures
$\epsh=(a,s,\vartheta)$. The in-plane pair is nonetheless reachable without any
fixture by rotating the specimen in the grips: with $\chi$ the angle between the
pull direction and the cut, a single tensile pull realises the ray
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\bm{n}}=(\hat a,\hat s)=(\sin\chi,\ \cos\chi),
\end{equation}
so that $\chi=0$ is pure shear along the ligament, $\chi=90^\circ$ is pure
opening, and intermediate angles are mixed states that additionally sample
different notch triaxialities --- which is precisely what is needed to calibrate
the pair $(\varepsilon_{f0},k)$ of the failure locus \eqref{eq:damage}. The
relative rotation $\vartheta$, the dominant degree of freedom during deployment,
is not accessible in force control; it is instead validated kinematically, by
folding a ligament to a prescribed angle and measuring the out-of-plane amplitude
of the buckle. The framing is therefore: \emph{calibrate in the accessible
in-plane modes, predict in rotation}, the same constitutive law serving both.

\paragraph{Domain truncation: the grip distance is a physical parameter.}
The representative volume element truncates the sheet at the window radius
$r_{\mathrm{win}}$, which is legitimate only if the disturbance created by the
ligament decays over that distance. It does not. A convergence study on a
$\wlig=18\,\mathrm{mm}$ ligament in shear gives a peak force that is logarithmic
in the truncation radius,
\begin{equation}
  F_s^{\max}\,[\mathrm{N}] \;=\; 303 - 41.2\,\ln\bigl(r_{\mathrm{win}}\,[\mathrm{mm}]\bigr),
  \qquad R^2=0.997 ,
  \label{eq:dcone-log}
\end{equation}
over $r_{\mathrm{win}}\in[27,108]\,\mathrm{mm}$. This is the signature of a
developable cone anchored at the cut tip, whose energy scales as
$B\theta^2\ln(R_{\mathrm{out}}/R_{\mathrm{core}})$ with $B$ the bending stiffness
and $\theta$ the cone angle, and whose displacement field decays as $1/r$: the
disturbance is long-ranged and there is no Saint-Venant plateau.
% CITE: developable cones, Cerda & Mahadevan; conical dislocations in thin sheets.
Two consequences follow. There is no converged domain size, so $r_{\mathrm{win}}$
must be reported with any ligament force; and a coupon gripped close to the
ligament measures a stiffer response than the same ligament embedded in a large
sheet --- truncating at $43\,\mathrm{mm}$ overestimates the peak force by more
than $60\%$ relative to the value extrapolated to the outer scale of the deployed
structure.
Comparison between experiment and simulation is therefore meaningful only when the
physical grip separation equals the simulated $r_{\mathrm{win}}$, and the fold
experiments below are run at $r_{\mathrm{win}}=100\,\mathrm{mm}$ on both sides. In
the deployed structure the outer scale is set instead by gravity, through the
elasto-gravitational length
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\mathrm{eg}}=\Bigl(\frac{B}{p}\Bigr)^{1/3},
  \qquad
  B=\frac{E t^{3}}{12(1-\nu^{2})},
  \qquad
  p=\rho g t,
  \label{eq:l-eg}
\end{equation}
equal to $0.18\,\mathrm{m}$ for the present sheet, at which
\eqref{eq:dcone-log} gives $F_s^{\max}\approx90\,\mathrm{N}$. The same
elasto-gravitational length governs the panel-tilting analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:buckling-locality}.

\paragraph{Ligament-level validation.}
Single-ligament coupons ($\wlig=18\,\mathrm{mm}$, $\beta=90^\circ$,
$\rho_f=0.16$, $t=0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$) cut from the same sheet were tested in fold
and in opening. In fold, the measured out-of-plane buckle height tracks the
simulated $u_z(\vartheta)$ up to $50^\circ$ with no material tuning --- the
imperfection amplitude, set to $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$, being the single adjusted
parameter --- and both saturate near $25\,\mathrm{mm}$ ($25.0\,\mathrm{mm}$
simulated against $25.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ measured). The measurement collapses onto
the one-parameter law
\begin{equation}
  u_z(\vartheta) = u_0\,\sqrt{\sin\vartheta},
  \qquad u_0 = 25.7\,\mathrm{mm},\quad R^2=0.97 ,
  \label{eq:buckle-law}
\end{equation}
which reproduces the square-root post-buckling scaling at small angles and
saturates at large angles as the finite ligament exhausts its developable-cone
reach; a two-parameter saturating exponential fits marginally better
($R^2=0.99$) but has no such interpretation. A simulated sweep over a fourfold
range of fillet ratios, $\rho_f\in[0.08,0.32]$, leaves the amplitude at
$u_0=29\pm1\,\mathrm{mm}$, so the fillet controls where the ligament tears but not
how far it buckles; the sharpest tip of that sweep is the least reliable, its
solve having diverged at $\vartheta=6^\circ$. The residual $13\%$ offset between
the simulated $u_0$ and the measured $25.7\,\mathrm{mm}$ is the current accuracy
of the buckling prediction.

In opening, the predicted and measured curves share the same signature --- elastic
rise, yield plateau, tearing drop --- with the model over-predicting the peak
force ($378\,\mathrm{N}$ against $233\,\mathrm{N}$). That comparison is
confounded, the same specimen having previously been folded to $90^\circ$ and
therefore being pre-creased, and is being repeated on virgin coupons; it is
reported here as an open item rather than as a calibration. Two caveats bound the
calibration as it stands. The load-bearing section is narrower than the nominal
ligament width, the fillet disc being centred on the cut tip so that the throat
width is $\wlig(1-\rho_f)=15.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ for the coupons above, and forces
must be normalised by that value and not by $\wlig$. And the coupons are
quasi-static ($1\,\mathrm{mm\,min^{-1}}$) whereas deployment proceeds at
centimetres per second, a regime in which PET stiffens and embrittles, so the
slowly measured fracture strain is optimistic and is carried with a rate margin.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Localisation of out-of-plane buckling in the ligaments}
\label{sec:buckling-locality}

Hypothesis (H2) asserts that the panels stay coplanar while the ligaments deform
out of plane. This section derives the condition under which that holds, and shows
that it is a competition between gravity acting on the panels and the bending
stiffness of the ligament. Throughout, $\wlig$ denotes the ligament width as
elsewhere in the paper, $t$ the sheet thickness, $Y=Et$ the stretching modulus,
$B=Et^{3}/[12(1-\nu^{2})]$ the bending stiffness, and $\theta$ the local fold
angle of the crease that forms in the ligament --- a quantity related to, but
distinct from, the hinge rotation $\vartheta$ of \eqref{eq:hinge-strain}.

In the thin-ligament regime $\wlig\gg t$, the tension-induced instability need not
tilt the panels out of plane: when they are held coplanar by gravity or by an
overburden, of combined areal load $p=\rho g t+q$, buckling can instead localise
in the ligaments. The selection between this localised mode and the panel-tilting
mode, together with the fold geometry, follows in closed form.

The relative motion of two adjacent panels shares only the primary-cut tip $O$,
and by Chasles' theorem a rigid motion with a fixed point is a rotation about it.
That $O$ is fixed follows energetically: translating it by $u$ strains the
junction, at a cost $\sim Y u^{2}$, whereas holding it fixed and folding costs only
bending, $\sim B\theta^{2}$; with $u\sim\theta\,\wlig$ the former exceeds the
latter by $12(1-\nu^{2})(\wlig/t)^{2}\gg1$. Symmetry about the fold plane
suppresses the remaining out-of-fold rotations. The fold apex localises at $O$
rather than in the ligament interior because a developable fold concentrates an
angle deficit $\sim2\theta$ that, in the interior, would form a disclination
requiring stretching, at a cost $\sim Y\theta^{2}\wlig^{2}$, whereas at the free
edge it is absorbed by the opening of the cut at no stretching cost --- that is,
by the cut-opening angle $2\theta_{o}$.

The bulge is therefore a developable cone anchored at $O$, the same object that
produces the logarithmic domain dependence \eqref{eq:dcone-log}. With $\wlig$ the
only available transverse length, its width is $\ell_f\simeq\wlig$. The idealised
cone's curvature discontinuities are smoothed over a bending boundary layer
$\ell_B=[B/(Y\theta^{2})]^{1/2}$; since the buckling angle is
$\theta\simeq\sqrt{2\varepsilon_c}=t/\wlig$, with
$\varepsilon_c\simeq\tfrac12(t/\wlig)^{2}$ the critical strain, one obtains
$\ell_B=\wlig/\sqrt{12(1-\nu^{2})}\approx0.3\,\wlig$, so that the fold is a single
smooth mound of finite curvature rather than a sharp crease.

Mode selection follows from a moment balance about the hinge axis of one panel of
edge length $L$. Gravity resists tilting with
$\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grav}}\simeq\tfrac12 p L^{3}$, while the folded ligament
drives it with $\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{el}}=E I_{O}\,\theta/\ell_f\simeq
Et^{4}/(12\,\wlig)$, where $I_{O}=\wlig t^{3}/12$. Their ratio is
\begin{equation}
\Gamma\equiv\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{grav}}}{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{el}}}
=\frac{6\,p\,L^{3}\,\wlig}{E\,t^{4}}
=\frac{1}{2(1-\nu^{2})}\left(\frac{L}{\ell_{\mathrm{eg}}}\right)^{3}\frac{\wlig}{t},
\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
with $\ell_{\mathrm{eg}}$ the elasto-gravitational length \eqref{eq:l-eg}. The
panels remain coplanar, and buckling localises in the ligaments, for $\Gamma>1$.
Taking $p=\rho g t$, the condition $\Gamma=1$ defines a critical panel edge length
\begin{equation}
  L^{\star}=\Bigl(\frac{E\,t^{3}}{6\,\rho\,g\,\wlig}\Bigr)^{1/3},
  \label{eq:l-star}
\end{equation}
in which the Poisson ratio cancels; panels larger than $L^{\star}$ stay flat and
panels smaller than $L^{\star}$ tilt. Values for several thin sheet materials are
given in Table~\ref{tab:critical-length}. The prefactor is of order unity and
follows from the choices made above, so $L^{\star}$ should be read as a scale
rather than as a threshold.

For the PET specimens of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}
($\wlig=18\,\mathrm{mm}$, panels of edge length $\sim0.5\,\mathrm{m}$),
\eqref{eq:gamma} gives $\Gamma\approx5\times10^{2}$ and
\eqref{eq:l-star} gives $L^{\star}\approx6\,\mathrm{cm}$, an order of magnitude
below the panel size: the panels are held flat by their own weight while the
ligament buckles freely, which is exactly the mechanism observed in the fold
experiments and assumed by the representative volume element, whose bounding
panel faces are rigid and coplanar. The single inequality $\wlig\gg t$ --- here
$\wlig/t=36$ --- underlies both the pinning of $O$ and the isometry of the fold;
as $\wlig\to t$ the junction stretches and peels, $O$ unpins, and the planar
auxetic response of the thick limit is recovered.
